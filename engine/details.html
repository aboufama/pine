Findings

    What is in a frame

    Thirty-one numbers, twenty times a second.

    The public sets were captured with reflective markers and cameras. CMU used 41 markers at 120 Hz; Bandai Namco delivers studio capture at 30 fps. Each take is solved to a skeleton, which we fit onto the robot. After that, every frame is the robot's own state: 23 joint angles, the direction of gravity seen from the pelvis, how fast the body turns, how fast it moves, and how high the pelvis is.

    Public motion capture never contains force or joint torque. A powered suit measures those from its own motors, for free, which is the one channel no public dataset can give a humanoid.

    Retarget

    One take, any humanoid.

    Pretrain

    A small transformer that learns how bodies move.

    Each token is one frame. The model reads 1.6 s of motion and predicts the next 200 ms of the whole body. During training, groups of sensors are hidden at random, so the same model learns to forecast and to fill in what a suit does not measure. No labels are needed, the same way language models learn from raw text.

    Next

    The same pipe on GPUs.

    Everything above ran on a two-core laptop-class CPU. The GPU version of the trainer is a PyTorch copy of this model, checked gradient for gradient against the CPU reference before it spends GPU time. It adds the larger public sets below, then trains a ladder of models up to a few tens of millions of parameters with a prediction registered before the largest run, as here.
